01

What the model actually is

It is not a search engine and it is not a person. It is a system that reads everything in the conversation so far, all of it at once, and produces the most likely next words. That "everything so far" is the context window. It is large, but it is finite, and when the conversation is over, it is gone.

You sit at a blank box and explain yourself, again, from scratch. The intelligence is extraordinary. The interface to it is a text field.

Three consequences follow. It does not remember last week unless you put last week in the window. It does not know your company unless your company is in the window. And if you ask the same question three times you can get three answers, because you are sampling, not looking something up.

Everything in this course is about what you put in the window, and how to stop putting it in by hand.

Do this · 5 min

Open the AI you use most. Ask it: "What do you know about my work?" Read the answer. Paste one line here about what it got wrong or did not know.

02

The five lines

Look at a real prompt. Not a demo, an actual one you'd send during work. Most of it isn't the question. It's setup.

You're a growth marketer. We sell ceramic cookware, tone is plain. I already tried a discount push, it flopped. Here's the spend file again. Never use emojis.

Five lines of context to earn one line of actual work: where did the money go? You wrote all five before. The model has been told all five before. Nothing carried over, so you pay the tax again.

The five lines are always the same five: who you are, the situation, what you already tried, the constraints, and the actual ask. Write them once, keep them in a file, paste the file. That is the whole of prompt engineering worth learning.

Do this · 15 min

Write your five lines for the job you do most weeks. Keep them under 80 words. Save them somewhere you can find on Monday.

03

Give it the file

Adjectives do not carry information. "Make it sound premium" tells the model nothing your competitor's brief did not also say. An example carries everything: three sentences you actually wrote, in the voice you actually want.

So: give it the file, not a description of the file. Give it the spreadsheet, not "our numbers are roughly flat". Give it last month's email that worked, not "write it like the ones that work".

Two habits. When you want the output in a shape, say the shape: a table with these columns, a list of five, one paragraph. And when you catch yourself typing an adjective, stop and paste an example instead.

04

Check it

The model produces the most likely next words. Likely is not the same as true. It will give you a confident citation that does not exist, a number that was never in the file, a name that sounds right. This is not a bug you can prompt away. It is what sampling is.

So you check, and you check the way an editor checks: the things that can be wrong. Numbers against the source. Names against the list. Claims against the document you gave it. Anything you would be embarrassed to have sent.

Two moves make this cheap. Ask it to quote the exact line it drew a claim from, so you can find it. And when it gets something wrong, do not say "try again"; say what was wrong, and give it the constraint or the example that fixes it. Retry gives you another sample. A correction gives you a better one.

Do this · 15 min

Give the AI a document you know well and ask it three factual questions about it. Ask it to quote the line for each answer. Note how many it got right.

Check

05

What never leaves

Everything you paste into a chat leaves your machine and goes to whoever runs the model. Sometimes that is fine. Sometimes it is a client's contract, a customer list, a password in a screenshot. You need a rule, and the rule has to be simple enough to follow at 11 pm.

The rule: if you would not email it to a stranger, do not paste it into a chat. For everything else, use the tools that keep the work on your side of the line: a local model for transcription and cleanup, an app that asks permission before it reads a folder, a setup where each app has to be granted access to each thing.

Keeping the context resident, keeping your data on your side of the line.

This is also why "connect everything to the AI" is the wrong goal. The right goal is: the AI can reach what you have allowed, and nothing else, and you can see the list.

Do this · 10 min

Write your rule in one line. Then list three things from your work that fail it.

06

Where prompting stops

A great prompt is evidence of a broken interface, not a skill worth having. It exists because the model can't see what you see, doesn't hold what you already told it, and can't act where the work actually lives.

There is a path, and it has stages. Prompted is where we are now: you carry everything to a blank box. Primed is the near future: the system already holds your context, your files, your standing intentions, so a nudge is enough. Promptless is the destination: the system sees the situation, understands what it takes, and acts before you have to assemble a request at all.

You have just spent three hours getting good at prompted. The next step is to stop: set the context up once, in a tool that holds it, and open an app for the job instead of a chat window. That is a day, in a room, with someone beside you. It is called Level 2.

The last prompt is the one you don't have to write.
