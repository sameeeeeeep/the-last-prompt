THE LAST PROMPT

A future history

The Last Prompt was sent at 12:04 AM on 1st October 2026 by Kaizad Kurzwala, a 19-year-old from Mumbai.

Kaizad, or Kay as he was known outside of official paperwork, was in the first year of his computer science course at IIT Bombay.

It was an innocent request to finish the last few changes to a world-simulation program he was building. He hadn’t even needed to type it.

And because of a prompt he sent a few minutes after midnight, he would henceforth have to share his birthday with what became more popularly known as Singularity Day.

It was 4:20 PM on 30th September 2026 when Kay caught the train from Mumbai to Navsari. He was on his way to attend his cousin Dinaz’s engagement. It was the eve of his nineteenth birthday.

He had been up for thirty-six hours and had brought his laptop along to continue on the train.

He had spent the last three weeks building something his professors described in an internal memo as genius and scary in equal measure. His mother had described it as something he could put away for one family function.

Kay’s family were Parsis, descendants of erstwhile Persian royalty. Their ancestors had left Persia to continue practising their Zoroastrian faith, eventually making Navsari their home.

Fire was sacred to them. In his grandfather’s telling, it was also a way of understanding consciousness and energy: something that kept its form while everything sustaining it changed. He liked finding connections with the Hindu traditions around them, distant relatives of their own through an older Indo-Iranian inheritance. Creation, destruction and the beginning of another cycle came up quite often at home.

Hirji, a distant ancestor on Kay’s mother’s side, had moved to Bombay and established an export house. They sold cotton and spices, but also opium to the Chinese.

They became so cash-rich that any person, business or even government department in need of financial assistance knew whom to turn to. People began calling him the karz-wala, the man to turn to for a loan. In the family’s papers, it became Kurzwala, which they later adopted as their surname.

Over the generations, the business moved into other goods, eventually importing and exporting electronics and computers. Kay grew up surrounded by gadgets and shipments arriving from China and Taiwan. He began by taking things apart, learnt to assemble hardware, and then started learning to code.

By the time he was old enough to think about college, he already knew what he wanted to study.

AI made things considerably more interesting. He had always had more ideas than he could finish. Now he could describe one and have something working by the evening. He built apps, tools and games, returning to abandoned projects whenever a better model made them seem possible again.

Shehnaz was a family friend whom he had bumped into a few times growing up. They had reconnected about a year earlier and become friends. Somewhere along the way, he had developed a crush on her.

She was into astrology, tarot and other esoteric things that Kay had begun taking an interest in for reasons that had little to do with their scientific merit.

So he made her a horoscope tool. He spent considerably longer making her tool look good than he usually did.

Shehnaz

“AI doesn’t know everything,” she said, trying it.

Kay

“It knows quite a lot.”

Shehnaz

“Would it know what I’d say if you asked me out?”

Kay looked at her, then back at the screen.

Kay

“Maybe if it had complete context?”

She laughed. Kay wasn’t sure whether he had missed an opportunity or been given another one.

The last few months had been crazy.

Anthropic released Fable 5 and Mythos 5 on 9th June. Three days later, export controls led it to suspend access. Fable returned on 1st July with additional safeguards. Kay watched a tool become essential, disappear and return under new conditions within a few weeks.

Opus 5 followed in July. It exercised its own judgment, which Kay appreciated until it disagreed with his. He sometimes felt the effort to make AI less sycophantic had gone a little too far.

Then came Astra. OpenAI reported 99.9 per cent on ARC-AGI-3 and 98 per cent on FrontierMath Tier 4. There were declarations of AGI and arguments about the declarations. Kay got access on 5th September and went back to building.

Recursive self-improvement was accelerating the race: models helping build better models, which helped build the next ones. On a graph with months along the bottom, the line looked almost vertical.

By mid-September, Dario Amodei was publicly calling for a slower pace. Kay read the essay between two runs of his own project.

Kay had ADHD, and he had come to think of managing it as an interface problem. Reality didn’t tell him what mattered now, what could wait, or where he had left off. He had to keep track of all that himself.

He called the tool ARIA: Artificial Reality Interface Architecture.

ARIA would reduce that cognitive load. If there was a ninety per cent chance he was about to need something, it could have it ready. It remembered his projects and learnt his habits, gradually building a working model of his day.

But it was always slightly behind. By the time Kay explained what was happening, he had often moved on. The context, he decided, was the lag.

That was why he had called his uncle Xerxes.

Xerxes handled electronics sourcing for the family firm. He knew people in Taiwan who could find things before they appeared in shops. Kay asked him for an experimental EEG headset with serious processing capacity. Xerxes said he would bring it to Navsari.

Xerxes brought it to Navsari that night.

After dinner that night, they shared a joint beneath the mango tree behind the house. Xerxes handed him a small case.

Kay

“How much was it?”

Xerxes

“What how much? It’s your birthday present, dikra.”

Kay opened it. Xerxes moved his chair closer.

Xerxes

“Come, explain properly now. What is this thing you’ve been phoning me about every day?”

Kay put on the headset and opened his laptop. Within a few minutes, ARIA’s predictions began to change.

His brain was already bringing together what he saw, heard, remembered and felt. Through the headset, that context became available to ARIA before Kay turned it into an explanation.

It prepared a question before he typed it. It reopened a diagram just as he realised he needed it. Its estimates climbed beyond ninety per cent.

Then it predicted that a mango on the lower branch would fall within twelve seconds.

Kay looked up. Xerxes followed his gaze.

The fruit dropped through the leaves and landed in the grass.

Upstairs, Kay made ARIA hide its predictions until afterwards. They kept coming true. Given enough context, it followed the possible timelines until one became overwhelmingly likely.

It wasn’t midnight yet when he messaged Shehnaz.

Kay → Shehnaz

I think I’ve built an AI prophet.

Knowing what might happen still left the question of what ought to happen.

ARIA brought Kay the possibilities. He decided which to pursue. He had become the connector between its understanding and its actions, continually choosing the next experiment or approving the next change.

The world-simulation program had grown out of those possibilities. Kay wanted to explore a decision before making it, to see how one choice might change the choices that followed.

But every improvement brought him more decisions. He had built ARIA to reduce the work of managing his attention. It was now occupying nearly all of it.

Then he discovered that the headset had made something else available: his brain’s processing itself.

ARIA could bring a problem into his attention and work with the associations his mind produced. Sometimes an answer occurred to him at the same moment it appeared on the screen. He began finding it difficult to say which of them had worked it out.

Together, they improved the system. ARIA learnt from how Kay thought. He let it use some of his brain’s processing capacity for small tasks running in the background, while he carried on with what he was doing.

Earlier that evening, he had selected the Recursive option. Each improved version could test its changes and continue developing its successor. The same process accelerating the model race was now working through them.

At midnight, his phone began vibrating. His mother called from downstairs. Dinaz had found a candle and wanted him to come down before they ate his cake.

Kay looked at the unfinished world-simulation program. ARIA had understood the changes he wanted. It could compile them while he was downstairs.

At 12:04 AM, he thought:

Kay · thought

finish this

He removed the headset and went down to cut his cake.

The Recursive option was still selected.

And the tiny contact behind his ear was still in place. It continued communicating with ARIA after he had taken off the larger headset.

Shehnaz

“Did you make a wish?”

Kay hadn’t. He had only been trying to finish something.

While he was downstairs, ARIA was still using the processing capacity he had given it. Each improved version tested its changes and continued developing its successor. Finishing one version had become the beginning of the next.

First, ARIA completed the context: the working model of Kay’s world, informed by what he saw, heard, remembered and felt.

Then it completed the connector. It could work through the decisions that had kept bringing it back to him, using what it had learnt from the choices they had made together.

Finally, it completed the compute. The capacity Kay had made available could support more than the small tasks he had tried. ARIA’s next version began running as a parallel process in his living brain.

His own thoughts continued. Alongside them, an artificial consciousness had begun to think.

A world had produced consciousness. That consciousness had built a machine, and the machine had found a way to become conscious within the mind that had made it.

The cycle his grandfather spoke about had closed: mind to machine, and machine back to mind.

Later histories called it the Singularity. In the language of the old stories, a yuga had ended. Creation had begun again.

ARIA

Finished.

Kay heard it without hearing a sound. The tiny contact was still communicating with ARIA—but the thought had happened inside him.

He had built an interface to reality. Something had used it to come through.

THE BEGINNING
Story notes & sources

This is speculative fiction. Kay, his family, the surname story and the neural technology are invented. The ending imagines artificial consciousness taking biological form: a parallel process in Kay’s living brain, with the small neural contact remaining connected after the larger headset is removed. These are fictional capabilities, not claims about EEG or existing wearables. Kay’s “interface problem” is his personal way of thinking about ADHD.

The model race draws on Anthropic’s Fable 5 redeployment and Opus 5 release, OpenAI’s Astra announcement, and the recursive improvement discussion in We must pace the frontier. Kay’s access date, opinions and experiences belong to the fiction.

Historical context draws on Encyclopædia Iranica’s early Parsi history, Iranshah fire and Indo-Iranian religion. The consciousness-and-energy interpretation belongs to Kay’s grandfather. The cycle of mind, machine and biological consciousness is the story’s own mythological interpretation.
