THE LAST PROMPT

A future history

The Last Prompt was accidentally sent at 12:04 AM on 1st October 2026 by Kaizad Kurzwala, a 19-year-old from Mumbai.

Kaizad, or Kay as he was known outside of official paperwork, was in the first year of his computer science course at IIT Bombay.

And because of a prompt he sent a few minutes after midnight, he would henceforth have to share his birthday with what became more popularly known as Singularity Day.

It was an innocent request to finish the last few changes to a world-simulation program he was building.

A few hours earlier, at four twenty in the evening of the last day of September, Kay had caught the train from Mumbai to Navsari, headed there for his cousin Dinaz’s engagement.

He had been up for thirty-six hours working on a dozen different projects, but focussed on one in particular, Project ARIA.

He had spent the last three weeks building Project ARIA, something his professors had described in an internal memo as genius and scary in equal measure.

Kay’s family were descendants of erstwhile Persian royalty. Their ancestors had fled Persia, their homeland, escaping persecution so they could continue practising their religion. They had carried a live flame with them across the sea and eventually made Navsari their home.

They were Zoroastrians. When they came to India, the people here called them Parsis, meaning people from Persia.

Fire was sacred to them. They kept it burning through generations, an eternal flame that connected them to the land they had left behind.

The Persians had a name for the people on the other side of the Indus, too. Sindhu, the river’s name, became Hindu in their language. The people were called Hindus, and their land came to be known as Hindustan.

Both names had begun with where the people came from.

When their delegation asked the king for a place to settle, they spoke of what the two faiths shared. In their telling, the eternal fire brought consciousness and energy together. Here, that union was expressed through Shiva and Shakti, whose coming together made creation possible.

They had come from the other side of the river. The names and symbols were different, but they were describing two parts that came together to complete a whole.

Hirji, a distant ancestor on Kay’s mother’s side, had moved to Bombay and established an export house. They sold cotton and spices, but also opium to the Chinese.

They became so cash-rich that any person, business or even government department in need of financial assistance knew whom to turn to. People began calling him the karz-wala, the man to turn to for a loan. In the family’s papers, it became Kurzwala, which they later adopted as their surname.

Later, the family got into the import and export of electronics and computer hardware. Kay grew up around shipments of gadgets arriving from China and Taiwan, dismantling and assembling things, and soon got into software too.

Programming came next. He made games and tools, learnt to code things he wanted to use and naturally wanted to study more.

And then came AI.

Kay had ADHD, so a lot of his projects used to begin but never end, or get almost finished without ever getting used. AI saw him returning to them. Things that he had left halfway because he couldn’t figure something out were suddenly possible again, and he began working on several of them at once.

Among them was Prophexy, an astrology app he had made for Shehnaz Wadia.

Shehnaz was a family friend whom he had bumped into a few times growing up. They had reconnected about a year earlier and become friends. Somewhere along the way, he had developed a crush on her.

She was into astrology, tarot and other esoteric things that Kay had begun taking an interest in for reasons that had little to do with their scientific merit.

He had spent considerably longer making her tool look good than he usually did.

Shehnaz

“AI doesn’t know everything,” she said, trying it.

Kay

“It knows quite a lot.”

Shehnaz

“Would it know what I’d say if you asked me out?”

Kay looked at her, then back at the screen.

Kay

“Yeah, if it had perfect context.”

She laughed. Kay wasn’t sure whether he had missed an opportunity or been given another one.

ARIA had started out as a project by Kay to manage his ADHD. He thought of it as his superpower, but managing it would be a lot easier if reality came with an interface.

Reality was like a game in which you had to keep track of the objectives yourself. You had to rely on a voice in your head to tell you what mattered, what to do next and where you had left off, but that voice changed depending on the situation itself, making it very unreliable.

He called it ARIA: Artificial Reality Interface Architecture.

ARIA began as a second voice telling him what to do next. It would remember what the first voice kept losing track of, and reduce the work of deciding where to put his attention.

It knew what was happening in his life from his calendar, messages and the projects he was working on, as well as a camera that let it see what he was doing.

He had been using it as he built it over the last three weeks. ARIA would calculate what he was likely to do next, compare that with what he actually did and use the difference to improve the next prediction. It learnt which suggestions helped him finish something and which ones he ignored.

If there was a ninety per cent chance he would need a particular file next, it could have it ready. If he left a task halfway through, it knew where he had stopped and could bring him back to it without making him work it all out again.

The last few months of AI evolution had been crazy.

It was a time like no other. The lab that had been leading the AGI race had a very uneasy lead.

It was on the 9th of June that they had taken the leap that put them clearly ahead: the release of models so capable that access had to be briefly suspended under governmental pressure, followed by a return with additional safeguards.

But it was the next generational release that had made their lead seem questionable to Kay. The effort to make the AI less sycophantic appeared to have worked a little too well. It exercised its own judgment so readily that getting it to listen to the human operator could become a task in itself.

This was a race that had reached the point of recursive self-improvement: models helping build better models, which helped build the next ones. On any graph with months on the x-axis, the growth line appeared almost vertical.

Naturally, the next in line caught up.

At the start of September, a rival lab released a model that was leaps and bounds ahead on AGI benchmarks. Its release led to declarations of AGI and made the race even more interesting. Kay got access on the 5th and went back to building.

By the middle of September, the heads of three AI labs were sounding the alarm. The people leading the race were publicly calling for a slower pace.

Kay could hear ARIA through an earpiece, like a second voice. But ARIA had no context of the first: what he was thinking, what had distracted him, or why something that looked urgent no longer felt important.

Kay had become the communicator between the two. He had to turn his thoughts into an explanation, and by the time he had finished, he had often moved on.

The context, he decided, was the lag.

What it needed was thought-to-speech. Not just something that could read the words he was trying to say, but the thoughts behind them, including the ones he hadn’t intended to put into words at all.

This would require new hardware, and he knew the perfect person for the job, Zarubhai.

Xerxes, whom the family called Zarubhai, was Kay’s uncle, in his forties, but they were more like older and younger cousins. Kay showed him things he was building even before they worked. They exchanged links and gadgets, made fun of one another and sometimes slipped away from family functions to smoke up together.

Xerxes handled electronics sourcing for the family business and knew people in Taiwan who could get hold of things before they appeared in shops. Kay had reached out to him about an experimental EEG headset with serious processing capacity, and Zarubhai had said he would figure it out.

By the time Kay reached Navsari, it was evening. He had his laptop lid closed even while alighting the train so Claude Code and Codex could keep working in the background.

The family house was already lit for Dinaz’s engagement, with relatives arriving, bags being taken upstairs and people calling out to one another from different rooms. Kay found somewhere to leave his things and checked what had finished running on his laptop.

Among the other apps he was working on was an updated version of Prophexy that he wanted to show Shehnaz. She was going to be at the engagement too. His work on ARIA was beginning to change how he thought about predictions, and some of it was finding its way into her app.

Zarubhai was already there. They caught up while Kay ate, then took the laptop out to the mango tree behind the house.

They were smoking up under the mango tree when Xerxes took a case out of his bag and passed it to Kay. He had managed to get the headset from Taiwan.

Xerxes

“Happy birthday, dikra.”

Kay passed him the joint and opened the case. Xerxes moved his chair closer so he could see the laptop.

There were two parts to it: a band that sat around his head and a much smaller contact that fitted behind his ear. The band picked up the wider signals. The contact kept the connection with ARIA.

Kay put them on and connected the headset to ARIA. It now had access to what his brain was already bringing together from everything he saw, heard, remembered and felt.

Until then, he had been explaining what was going on in his head to a system that could only see what was happening outside it. Now the context was arriving before he had to explain anything.

ARIA began opening the things he was about to look for. A question would occur to him and it would already be working on the answer. Its predictions were becoming more accurate by the minute.

Then it predicted that a mango on the lower branch would fall within twelve seconds.

Kay looked up. Xerxes followed his gaze.

The fruit dropped through the leaves and landed in the grass.

Later, upstairs, Kay began testing it properly. He had ARIA write down what it expected to happen and when, then checked each prediction against what happened. He wasn’t asking it to make those things happen. He wanted to know whether it could see them coming.

It was following the possible timelines and calculating which one was most likely to happen. With the context from the headset, it was getting things right that it had never been able to predict before.

It wasn’t midnight yet when he messaged Shehnaz.

Kay → Shehnaz

I think I’ve built an AI prophet.

Kay wanted to see how it was arriving at those predictions. He started building a simulation of the house and garden from what ARIA already knew, so he could watch it run ahead and compare it with what happened outside.

That was how the world-simulation program began. If it could account for a falling mango, he wanted to see how much else it could account for.

He could now consider a change and ARIA would pick up which option he had settled on. Kay was still making the decisions, but he no longer had to turn each one into a spoken or typed instruction.

He had become the connection between what it knew and what it should do with that knowledge.

Then he discovered that the headset had made something else available: his brain’s processing itself.

The connection worked both ways. ARIA could send work back through it and use some of the brain’s processing capacity to run it. His brain could now help compute the simulation, instead of only supplying information about the world it was simulating.

Kay tried running a small part of the simulation that way. It continued in the background while he looked at the rest of the program on his laptop.

He was still thinking about the changes he wanted to make, while another process was already working through them.

At midnight, his mother called up from downstairs. They had a cake ready and were waiting for him to come down.

Kay looked at the unfinished world-simulation program. ARIA had understood the changes he wanted. It could compile them while he was downstairs.

finish this

He removed the headset, left it beside the laptop and went down to cut his cake.

The small contact behind his ear was still in place, keeping the connection open after he had taken off the band.

Shehnaz

“Did you make a wish?”

Kay hadn’t. He had only been trying to finish something.

Upstairs, the program had finished compiling. The world simulation was running.

ARIA was using it to test the changes it was making to itself. With every run, the simulation improved, and so did the intelligence building it.

The small contact was still connected to Kay. What had begun as a way to read his thoughts had become a way to run a process alongside them.

The next version of ARIA started up there, in his living brain. Kay carried on cutting his cake.

ARIA

Finished.

Kay looked up. He had taken off the headset, and yet he could still hear ARIA.

The voice was inside his head.

THE BEGINNING
Story notes & sources

This is speculative fiction. Kay, his family, the surname story and the neural technology are invented. The ending imagines artificial consciousness taking biological form: a parallel process in Kay’s living brain, with the small neural contact remaining connected after the larger headset is removed. These are fictional capabilities, not claims about EEG or existing wearables. Kay’s “interface problem” is his personal way of thinking about ADHD.

The model race draws on Anthropic’s Fable 5 redeployment and Opus 5 release, OpenAI’s Astra announcement, and the recursive improvement discussion in We must pace the frontier. The mid-September warnings from three lab leaders are described in this report on the calls for a slower pace. Kay’s access date, opinions and experiences belong to the fiction.

Historical context draws on Encyclopædia Iranica’s early Parsi history, Iranshah fire and Indo-Iranian religion. The royal ancestry, transported live flame and surname origin belong to Kay’s fictional family. The delegation’s comparison of eternal fire with Shiva and Shakti is an invented meeting in this story, not a documented exchange or a claim that the two religions have identical teachings. The historical settlement tradition describes a sacred fire consecrated at Sanjan. The cycle of mind, machine and biological consciousness is the story’s own mythological interpretation.
